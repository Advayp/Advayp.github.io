One of my favorite quotes from this book goes something along the lines of "to ask what is the meaning of life is to ask what the best move in chess is." Our life's meaning is something that can change from time to time. What's important to recognize, though, is that we're *burdened* with the responsibility of being the only person that can realize our meaning. Frankl contends that this is the "why" of our life: each individual has some greater purpose(s) that only they can fulfill. We must reorient ourselves towards finding this meaning and realizing it.

Frankl says there's three main types of meaning:
1. The kind that involves performing some act or piece of work. This is the most common and well-understood kind.
2. The kind that involves love towards someone. Frankl presents love towards someone as not attraction or emotional connection, but a lens into that person's meaning with the goal of helping them realize it. Love is one of the most powerful forms of meaning there is, being able to exist without reciprocation or certainty and enabling perseverance of the most extreme suffering.
3. The kind that involves suffering. Specifically, unavoidable suffering towards some greater "why" is meaningful, and, almost paradoxically, this meaning helps us endure the suffering in the first place. Having some greater purpose helps us shift our attitude towards any suffering we face when chasing it. By imagining ourselves as having realized that purpose, we act as any suffering we face is a thing of the past. In his discussion, Frankl adopts a stoic attitude towards suffering and our circumstances in general, saying that, no matter what, we always have the freedom to choose our attitude towards our immediate circumstance. 

Reading this fundamentally shifted how I thought about meaning. I had never really thought about meaning as something dynamic, and had never considered being "the only" as a defining characteristic for meaning. The search for meaning itself can be meaningful, as you're the only person that can find a meaning fit for yourself. The main consequence of such a shift is that I no longer see meaning as something elusive I spend my whole life looking for, only succeeding when I'm on my death bed. Rather, I can recognize that we're surrounded by meaning, and just maybe unaware of it at times.

Another key concept I'll take away from this book is the idea of a tragic optimism (optimism in the face of tragedy) that follows a discovery of meaning. Optimism isn't something that can be pursued, it is something that ensues as a result of meaning. With this optimism, we can face any pain, guilt, death, or any tragedy we encounter. Meaning empowers us to deal with anything that comes in our way of realizing it.
