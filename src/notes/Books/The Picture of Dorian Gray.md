This book to me felt more like an exploration of a philosophy than a traditional book. Wilde criticizes hedonism by exploring how Dorian Gray changes after requesting the portrait grow old instead of him. Specifically, Wilde emphasizes the nature of hedonism to pervert the mind into a selfish, narcissistic, sinful entity, one that is ultimately incapable of love and that can only focus on the short-term. We're only cognizant of this because of Wilde's omniscient narration, which offers a glimpse into Dorian's mind throughout the novel. We get to see its innocence at the beginning, and how it fully falls apart over the course of the story. What's interesting is that, although Dorian changes significantly, there's always a part of him that resents / regrets what he's doing and the person he's become. 

I think it's interesting to read this book now, at a time when dopamine is so easily accessible. Even though our life's aim may not explicitly hedonism, turning to pleasure is more attractive and accessible than it's ever been historically. While modern pleasures themselves differ from the ones mentioned in the novel, the warning remains timeless.
