The Count of Monte Cristo was easily my longest read ever, and one of the first books I picked up in an attempt to start reading again. Following the story of Edmond Dantes, a sailor who loses everything due a false accusation, the book conducts a symphony between the instruments of revenge and love, with notes on religion sprinkled in between.

One of my biggest takeaways from the novel is Dumas' criticism of revenge. Dantes, through his various disguises, carries out a master plan to take revenge on four people who wronged him at the very beginning. But, in doing so, he takes it too far, affecting innocent people, which ironically, is what he was so vehemently against. Moreover, he loses who he was. He's not Dantes anymore - he's the Count of Monte Cristo or Abbe Busoni or Lord Wilmore or whatever disguise he chose to have on. His overwhelming quest for revenge, which he believes is the word of God, destroys his identity and consumes him to the point of perverting his nature. He performs good acts, not because they're inherently good, but because they contribute to his scheme.  One moment that stood out to me was when Dantes had to collect and prepare himself before facing the Morrel family, who was described as being harmonious and benevolent. 

Besides just consuming him, Dantes' quest for revenge ends up claiming many innocent lives. This collateral damage is intentional on Dumas' part, demonstrating the negative consequences revenge can have. 

Regarding love, Dumas contrasts two main relationships: Dantes and Mercedes, and Valentine and Maximillian. The main difference in these two love stories is that Valentine and Maximillian refuse to live in a world without the other, literally preferring death. Mercedes, on the other hand, was eventually able to move on from Dantes, thinking he was dead. When Maximillian thought Valentine was dead, he was willing to commit suicide to reunite with her. Through this comparison, Dumas indicates that true love can only prosper with a significant level of commitment (to the point of death), and is immaterial. It is one thing to say that you will die for someone, and another to actually do so.

The last moment of the novel that really stood out to me was Dantes' final words to Maximillian, which Dumas uses as an opportunity to include some philosophy in the novel. Dumas states that there is neither happiness nor unhappiness in the world, only the comparison of one state with another. To experience the highest of highs you must have gone through the lowest of lows. Dumas also mentions that the sum of human knowledge is contained in the words "wait" and "hope". To me, these final words in the novel say that persistence through suffering brings happiness, and, in some sense, the longer the suffering, the more extreme the happiness. To end off the tale of Dantes' suffering with a note about its value is especially powerful, as you, the reader, are right there with Dantes all along the way. You see what he has, what he loses, how he deals with his problems. This last section of the novel was particularly inspiring and satisfying.
